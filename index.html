The rigging system

One hook.
One load path.

A rigged load is a single connected load path -- every component between the hook and the load carries the lift, and the weakest or most misapplied link governs the whole assembly. Capacity depends on the hitch, included angle, hardware, bearing surfaces, and center of gravity, all working within the marked capacity on the sling tag and hardware.
Orientation only. This guide does not replace the sling identification tag, manufacturer capacity data, applicable inspection criteria, lift plan, or direction from a qualified person. Actual capacities vary by manufacturer, construction, grade, configuration, and condition.

Teach the six decisions

Ask first, listen for evidence, then coach the decision. Reveal the instructor lens only after the room has committed to an answer. These lenses support instruction; they do not disclose the secured knowledge-check answer key.

01

Establish the load

Competency: distinguish verified load information from assumptions before selecting equipment.

Ask the room“What do we know, what are we assuming, and what would make us stop before connection?”
Listen forLoad and rigging weight, center of gravity, approved attachment points, travel path, landing surface, environment, people, and contingencies. Coach the stop: unknown weight, uncertain lift point, or a trial-lift response that contradicts the plan means lower and reassess.
02

Select the hitch

Competency: choose a hitch that controls the load and matches the sling’s identified rating.

Ask the room“What does this hitch control, and what new failure exposure does it introduce?”
Listen forContainment, balance, possible slippage or rotation, choke angle, contact damage, and the specific vertical, choker, or basket rating on the identification. Coach the why: hitch capacity and behavior are not interchangeable.
03

Size at the working angle

Competency: calculate theoretical demand, state assumptions, and verify identified capacity.

Ask the room“Whose share of the load are you calculating, and what assumption could make that share wrong?”
Listen forShare of load, angle measured from horizontal, load-angle factor, equal effective leg length, centered CG, and conservative treatment of three- or four-leg systems. Finish with the sling tag and actual configuration—not the classroom number alone.
04

Select and verify hardware

Competency: trace force through marked, compatible hardware loaded in its intended direction.

Ask the room“Where is the force actually bearing, and what part is being bent, spread, or side loaded?”
Listen forIdentification, pin seating, bow and pin orientation, hook bowl loading, throat/latch condition, side-load limits, eye and pin fit, and manufacturer data. Appearance is not a capacity decision.
05

Protect the load path

Competency: identify every sling contact and control cutting, abrasion, crushing, heat, and small-radius bending.

Ask the room“Where will this sling move or tighten during the first inch of lift?”
Listen forEdges on both sides of the load, bearing radius and D/d, protection compatibility, movement during tensioning, heat or chemical exposure, and whether the protection stays in place. Hands stay out while the sling tightens.
06

Verify and control the lift

Competency: coordinate people, communication, trial lift, travel path, and landing.

Ask the room“What must be true before the operator continues past the trial lift?”
Listen forClear roles and signals, controlled fall zone, balanced load, seated connections, stable rigging, clear path, useful taglines, ready landing and blocking, and a shared stop signal. Tilt, shifting, lost communication, fouled control line, or unexpected movement means stop and lower.

Four field stations

Rotate groups of four to six learners. Require every person to point to evidence and explain the decision. Do not let the most experienced participant make every call for the group.

Calculate and challenge

Control the first inch

Use a tabletop model or no-load role-play to rehearse communication, fall-zone control, trial lift, and landing.

RolesSignalsStop work
  • Assign operator interface, signal person, rigger, and spotter roles.
  • Mark the fall zone and planned travel path.
  • Introduce one event: tilt, shift, lost signal, fouled tagline, or unready landing.
  • Require a clear stop, lower, reassess, and communication sequence.
Stop coaching point: no learner reaches under, steadies by hand, or enters a simulated fall zone to “fix it quickly.”

Debrief for transfer

A good debrief does not ask only whether the answer was right. It makes the crew explain the evidence, the failure exposure, the decision, and the stop condition they will carry into work.

After every activity

  1. What did you observe—not assume?
  2. What could fail, move, release, cut, or overload?
  3. Which document, marking, or person controls the decision?
  4. What action follows: continue, correct, remove, or escalate?
  5. What condition makes the crew stop or lower the load?

Capstone challenge

  1. Which part of your plan is most sensitive to a wrong assumption?
  2. How would an offset center of gravity change the load share?
  3. What will you verify during the trial lift?
  4. Who has authority to stop, and how is that stop communicated?
  5. What must be ready before the load travels or lands?

Watch for misconceptions

  1. “Four legs means four equal shares.”
  2. “A bigger shackle always solves the connection.”
  3. “The latch carries the load.”
  4. “A soft sling does not need edge protection.”
  5. “The calculated number is the sling rating.”

Close the qualification gap

  1. Ask each learner to name the limits of today’s evidence.
  2. Identify task-specific OJT or supervised practice still required.
  3. Record equipment or scenarios the learner has not demonstrated.
  4. Reinforce that employer assignment depends on the specific load, lift, equipment, knowledge, and experience.
Open-source training basisOSHA recommends activity-based, participatory learning and worksite demonstrations. OSHA also states that completing a course does not by itself make a person qualified for every rigging task; the employer evaluates qualification for the specific lift.
